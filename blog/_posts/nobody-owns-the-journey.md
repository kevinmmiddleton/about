I have asked some version of the same question at every company I've worked at. Who owns the customer lifecycle, end to end?

Nobody has ever raised their hand.

Not once. Plenty of people own a stage of it, and they own their stage well. But the whole arc, from the first search to the repeat purchase years later, belongs to no one in particular at most companies I've seen.

## The clearest version I ever saw

A heating and cooling system lasts 10 to 15 years. When a homeowner buys one, they're starting one of the longest product relationships in their life. Longer than their car. Much longer than their phone.

I worked on HVAC.com, part of Trane Technologies, and I'm proud of what we built there. But the thing I keep coming back to isn't something we shipped. It's that question, and the silence after it.

Walk through what a homeowner actually goes through. They research a problem. They make a repair-or-replace decision. They get quotes, pick a contractor, live through install day. They register the warranty, or mean to. They download an app, or don't. They buy filters, schedule maintenance, weather a breakdown or two. And then, ten or fifteen years later, they do the whole thing again.

That's one relationship. The org chart sees it as eight.

A decade is an extreme case, which is exactly why it's useful. It makes visible a gap that exists at almost every company, including ones where the whole lifecycle fits in a quarter.

## The customer doesn't experience your org chart

Marketing owns awareness. Product owns the tool. Sales owns the lead. Support owns the ticket. Operations owns fulfillment. Every one of those teams can be excellent and the experience between them can still be terrible.

You feel it as a customer. Handoffs are clunky. Context gets lost. You end up with separate logins for things that should obviously know about each other. The journey feels stitched together, because it was.

The customer doesn't experience your org chart. They experience the seams.

With a decade-long product, the seams get expensive. There are years of quiet between purchases where nobody's number depends on staying in touch, so by the time the next unit is due, the brand is starting from scratch with someone who has been their customer for fifteen years.

## Ask a Lawyer had two of everything

At Rocket Lawyer we had an Ask a Lawyer experience that was split down the middle. The US version routed to Salesforce. The UK version routed somewhere else entirely. Same product name, same promise to the customer, two different machines underneath.

The reasoning was always historical. That's how each one got built, and each one worked for the market it was built in.

My read was that a routing API would have solved it. Send the request to the right place based on where the customer is, and Ask a Lawyer stops being two regional implementations and starts being one product. Portable. Reusable. Something you could put behind a partner's brand, or extend into use cases nobody had scoped yet, because the plumbing no longer cared who was asking.

I heard some consolidation happened years later.

The part that stayed with me is that nothing about that was a hard technical problem. It stayed split because no single person was looking at Ask a Lawyer as one experience with one direction. Everyone was correctly maintaining their half.

## The real cost is that you never get to be strategic

Here's what I think the seams actually cost, and it isn't only the customer.

When teams own segments, every roadmap conversation happens inside a segment. You optimize your stage, you hit your stage's numbers, and you ship features that make your slice better. That's execution mode, and it can look extremely productive for years.

What you can't do from inside a segment is make a bigger bet. You can't decide that the thing most worth doing this year is the six months between purchase and first maintenance, because nobody owns that stretch and no roadmap has a line for it. The strategic move requires someone with a view of the whole thing, and there is no such person.

So the org stays busy. Retention sags, NPS goes flat, and the post-mortems blame price or the market. Nobody's dashboard shows how many customers fell into the gaps between teams, because no dashboard is drawn that way.

Product folks usually see this first. We sit closest to the seams, we get asked to coordinate across them, and we're rarely given the authority to decide anything about them. If you've ever owned a product that was working while the journey around it was failing, you know the exact feeling.

## What actually changes it

I've only seen two structures genuinely close the gap.

Someone gets real cross-functional authority over the end to end. A general manager, a chief customer officer, somebody who can force the conversation when the warranty team and the app team have never met. A title without the authority doesn't count.

Or the company goes product-led, where the experience is the product, and product defines the journey that every other function supports.

Most companies live in the middle, where product sees everything, owns nothing, and gets blamed when the handoffs fail.

## What you can do without being handed the authority

You probably can't restructure your company this quarter. You can do this.

**Draw the whole lifecycle on one page.** First search to repeat purchase, however many years that spans. Not the funnel your team owns. The whole thing, the way the customer lives it.

**Mark every handoff between teams.** Then circle the ones where nobody would notice if the customer disappeared. Those circles are your argument.

**Find the one stretch worth targeting.** Don't propose owning everything, because that's a reorg and it isn't yours to call. Propose that this specific gap is where the next material improvement lives, and bring the evidence.

**Ask the question out loud, in a room with the people who own the pieces.** Who owns the end to end? Watch what happens. In my experience the room goes quiet, and that silence does more to move people than any deck I could have built.

The HVAC.com work that moved the most was exactly this. The site had two million annual visitors converting at 0.1%, mostly landing on blog posts. We A/B tested our way to marginal gains until we asked what the homeowner was actually doing, which was standing in front of a dying furnace trying to decide whether to repair or replace it. The interactive calculators we shipped to answer that question converted at 50 times the blog baseline. That didn't come from any one team's swim lane. It came from looking at the journey the way the customer lived it and finding the spot where they were ready to move.

That was one stretch of the decade. Nobody owned making it the whole thing.

## Ask it anyway

If you take one thing from this, make it the question. Ask who owns the customer end to end, in a room, out loud, with the people who own the pieces.

If the answer is "everyone," the real answer is no one. And your customers already know it, because they're the only ones living the whole decade.
