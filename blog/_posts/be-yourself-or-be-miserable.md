Everyone is rushing to make themselves fit into boxes. Then we work for companies that don't have a mission, and wonder why we get laid off so carelessly.

I don't think this is a new problem, but I do think it's becoming more and more obvious to people. A lot of people in tech that I know, especially engineers, are having an identity crisis right now. What am I at work if I can be replaced? With all these AI layoffs, what is anyone worth?

So you start to think about the things you give up to show up fully at work. What do you turn off? What are you code switching to be a certain way at work, to get through the day and make a living in what seems to be a world changing faster than it ever has?

### You can do anything for a year

When my mom first told me you could do anything for a year, it was my first job after college. I interviewed with a couple of consulting firms and made it to the end on one. I didn't quite get it. So I emailed the recruiter every week to keep the line open, and I think eventually she just wanted me to stop emailing. Hey, I'll take any victory I can get. Kind of sums up my whole career.

I had told her originally I wasn't willing to work in rural Virginia, because I wanted to work in D.C. Around my fourth or fifth email (I think I wished her a happy new year), she called and asked if I was willing. I flipped and told her I was. That was a big blow, because I had a different life in mind for myself. My mom helped me move to Abingdon, Virginia, and she said, you can do anything for a year.

Just shy of two years later, I got a job in D.C. and moved to Arlington. So it all ended up working out.

I thought about that when I saw something on Blind the other day. Somebody said they're extremely stressed at work, but they make over $700K, and asked whether or not they should quit.

My advice was the same thing my mom told me: you can do anything for a year. What a privilege to be making so much money. Do it for a year, then move somewhere you can be more grounded. Take the money you save and put it away as a nest egg or a down payment on a house.

But so often we're chasing big paychecks or prestige. This person took a big job, and they already didn't want it.

### Focus on what you can control

Knowledge work is difficult. Keeping a consistent job in tech is difficult. If you look at all the layoffs, it's difficult to have tenure at a tech company right now.

So if you can prioritize one thing, prioritize your day-to-day happiness and health. That's the one thing you have the most control of.

You can't control the project you work on, if the company mission changes, if there are layoffs, or if you get a new manager. But you can control your day-to-day happiness.

### Be yourself now or pay later

Maybe you're interviewing and trying to come off like a go-getter who works [9-9-6](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/996_working_hour_system) or you're trying to come off like you really fit the role (when you repeatedly held back eye-rolls during the interview). Then you get hired, and congratulations, you're on a time clock, because eventually who you really are and what you really want from your career is guaranteed to come out, even if only in a rare moment of frustration.

For all of us, when it comes to work, it's either going to work with the culture or it won't. And if it doesn't, you're going to want to leave, no matter what you said in the beginning.

What we each want, our personal minimum requirements, are pretty well set. Our needs and preferences may shift a little, because we all change over time, but the core of what we each want is probably going to stay fairly stable.

When I'm asked what I look for in a team, I say **people**, **process**, and **product**. Great people that collaborate and are open enough to talk about and improve the process of how we build great product. If you have a truly engaged team, access to the right levers to build and measure, and people who are working together on a shared mission, is there anything you _can't_ do? I don't think so.

### Is my weird compatible with yours?

If you're looking for a job, or for a specific team inside the company you already work for, what you should really be looking for is where you can be yourself and maintain a level of happiness. Truly bring yourself to work, and optimize for the things you care about. Is it a collaborative culture? People who are really care? People you can learn something from? Just an overall good vibe? We spend at least 40 hours a week at work, so this is an important part of your life.

For me, bringing myself to work is knowing that everybody's a little bit weird. So is my weird compatible with this group? And is this group going to be supportive?

We never want anything bad to happen. But if something catastrophic happened, either personally or professionally, would this group have my back and lift me up?

A few years ago, I ended a hiring manager interview at minute seven. She never said hi or told me her name, and she said our conversation "wouldn't take the whole time." When I started talking about my experience, she interrupted me. She was going to be my manager, and someone treating me this way at the hiring manager stage was absolutely not going to advocate for me, care about me, or care about my psychological safety at work. I knew immediately this relationship was not going to go well if this is how she was greeting me. So I left the call.

I told the recruiter I'd ended the call early and wasn't interested. Then I wrote to the CEO, because an empathetic leader is table stakes for me in every role going forward.

Then I forwarded it to a friend with a note: "oops I did it again."

Ultimately, that's what we're all looking for, I think. We're looking for psychological safety. We're looking for the ability to make mistakes and recover from them. And we're looking to grow. And If the answer is no, I'm not in a place where I feel supported, I assume you're already one foot out of the door.

### The one that got away

Here's the 360 perspective. In 2021, I picked Sendoso, a Series B startup, over Salesforce. The hiring team at Salesforce, on MuleSoft, wanted me so badly. They set up a call with me and begged me to reconsider. And the people at Sendoso felt so genuine. Honestly, both teams were, which made it incredibly difficult. But something about the team at Sendoso swooned me and put me just over the edge.

I even made a spreadsheet: 23 weighted factors, from "Company Reputation" to "Excitement." Sendoso won, 298.5 to 290.5. Looking at it now, I was just trying to justify my decision. Part of the reason I said no was that I didn't want to be, quote, a product manager of a marketing site. Which is so ridiculous, because I went on to do amazing work at HVAC.com later.

Sendoso struggled to be profitable. I could see changes coming, with a recession on the way, so I left for Lever, and within two months there were massive layoffs. Looking back, I think it would have been the right choice to take the Salesforce job. The hiring manager kept in touch, and a year after I didn't take the role, he told me I was the one that got away. That was such a huge compliment.

But I don't really look back in regret. I've learned from every mistake I've made. Hindsight's 20/20, and you never know how things are going to work out. I think about that time occasionally, and fondly.

### The job hopper myth

There's this through line, especially in tech, that nobody wants to say anywhere. That's ridiculous. If you look at companies who are known for shorter tenures, you'll see a [PIP](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Performance_improvement#Performance_Improvement_Plans_\(PIPs\)) culture, [where they're always culling the bottom 20%](https://www.reddit.com/r/antiwork/comments/1ejt0d8/the_inhuman_pip_culture_of_meta_and_amazon/), or an environment that isn't conducive for people with families, or don't allow for internal transfers to continue career growth within the company. Maybe you do great work in one area of the company, and you see another area open up and you want to try it out. Maybe you try it, fail, and go back to your old area. Or you try it, fail, and move to another part of the company. I don't see enough companies championing this!

There's a lot of criticism that we don't spend a long time at companies. I reject that. If companies offer enough enrichment, things to learn and areas to grow, there's nothing wrong with staying at the same company.

That goes with the kind of company you're building and the kind of people you want to attract. It means recognizing the talents of the people you already have, and enabling them to try new things.

A great fit of a role, with a team that gets it and gets you. I think that's what we're all looking for, ourselves and the teams we'll join.
