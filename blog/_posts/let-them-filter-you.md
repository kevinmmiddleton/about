Someone once told me that companies are mostly looking for reasons to filter candidates out.

And what?

Let them.

Here's what optimizing against that actually produces. You sand off every edge that might catch on something. The right keywords, the clean resume, answers calibrated to give nobody a reason to say no. What's left is a candidate who clears every bar and stays in nobody's memory.

I'd rather get filtered out in week one than month six.

## The version of you that gets hired has to show up on Monday

This is the part the advice skips.

If you interview as a smoother, quieter, more agreeable version of yourself, that person is who they hired. You can keep performing them for a while. Most people can hold it for a few months.

Then your actual self arrives, because it always does. Either they're fine with it, or you are back exactly where you started, except now it cost you six months, a resignation, and the story you have to tell about why you left so fast.

Avoiding the hard question didn't remove the risk. It moved the risk somewhere more expensive and made you pay it later.

## The power dynamic is real. The right place doesn't act like it.

I'm not going to pretend the power dynamic is fair. It isn't. They have the job, set the timeline, usually know more about you than you know about them, and if you need the income none of that is abstract. The power imbalance is real.

But in a healthy process the company understands it's being evaluated too, and it acts like it. They answer your questions because they would rather you opt out in October than quit in March.

A place that treats a reasonable question as insubordination is giving you a preview of the job.

Pay attention to how you feel as it goes on, too. The further into a process that isn't right for you, the harder you have to work to perform, so don't ignore those signals. Being nervous is normal, but being nervous about asking a reasonable question is worth examining.

## I handed my interviewer a list of things I hate doing

When I was interviewing at HVAC.com, I shared my Mnookin Two-Pager with Matt Triplett, who became my manager.

It's an exercise from phyl terry's *Never Search Alone*, named for Alison Mnookin: you write down what you love doing, what you hate doing, your must-haves and your must-nots, and then you hand it to people and ask whether it sounds like you.

Giving that to a hiring manager is not the advice. It's too honest and too early, and it hands someone a tidy list of reasons to pass on you.

It's the reason he hired me.

He told me later that I'd talked about giving grace to get grace, and it stuck with him. That is what moved me to the top of the list. Not keyword optimization. Not a performance of the ideal candidate. Just being clear about how I actually work.

That's getting filtered in.

## The questions cost less than the regret

Every interview has a question you want to ask and talk yourself out of. How does this team handle disagreement. What happened to the last person in this role. What does a bad week look like here. Is this a rebuild or a rescue.

You are going to learn the answer either way. The only variable is whether you learn it before or after you've signed.

So ask. If a clarifying question is what ends it, sit with what that tells you. A team that reads curiosity as a threat during the part of the process where everyone is on their best behavior is not going to get more relaxed about it once you're on payroll. That's not a rejection so much as a preview.

And when they ask you something, answer it honestly, within reason. Nobody needs your whole interior life in a 45-minute call. But don't invent a person who wants things you don't want. You'll have to keep being them.

## You can stage the hard ones

Asking everything in the first screen is a lot, and it isn't necessary. There's a sequence that works.

Early rounds are for how the work actually happens. How decisions get made, who owns what, what the last hard call was and how it went. These are cheap to ask and enormously revealing, and no reasonable interviewer flinches at them.

Later rounds are for the specifics of the job. Scope, support, what success looks like at six months, what's actually broken that they hope you'll fix.

Offer stage is where the rest belongs. Comp, flexibility, boundaries, whatever your must-nots are. You have the most room to negotiate after they've decided they want you, and the questions that feel riskiest cost the least right there.

Holding a question for the right moment is judgment. Never asking it is something else.

## What you actually get for it

I've written before about how [curiosity is the difference between thriving at a company and surviving it](https://middleton.io/blog/curiosity-makes-or-breaks/), and how quickly you can tell which one you're in. The interview is your first read on that, and it's the cheapest one you will ever get. Nobody has committed anything yet.

Use it. Watch what happens when you ask something slightly inconvenient. Notice whether they answer or manage you. That reaction is data you cannot get from the job description, the Glassdoor reviews, or the recruiter's pitch.

Being filtered out on that basis is the system working. It found the mismatch on day one instead of month six, at no cost to you beyond a few hours and some disappointment.

The place that's right for you will read the same questions as a good sign. Those are the people you want to work for anyway.

So ask. It might take longer to land. You will not regret it, and how you show up now is how you'll be expected to show up later, so you may as well start as yourself.
