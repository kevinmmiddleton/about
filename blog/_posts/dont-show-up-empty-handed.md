If you created the meeting and showed up with no agenda, no point of view, and no quick notes for anyone to browse as you kick it off, you shouldn't have scheduled it. You're not ready.

[![Miranda Priestly in The Devil Wears Prada, at a run-through where nothing is ready, holding a dress up next to an assistant.](/blog/images/why-is-no-one-ready.jpg "Miranda Priestly: “You people have had hours and hours to prepare.” Why is no one ready?")](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=-rDTRuCOs9g)

With the current AI tools available, there's absolutely no reason for everyone not to show up prepared. These tools make it incredibly easy to create quick agendas, summarize notes, and come ready. We had excuses before, so the best of us, the more thoughtful of us, would come prepared. Now there's no excuse for everyone not to. And if you're not showing up prepared, what are you doing?

I joke about meetings with 20 people in attendance and maybe one or two people actually "doing work". A lot of watchers, not a lot of doers.

In some organizations, I felt like we were all rowing in the same direction. Marketing would say, "I haven't heard from you yet." Design would say, "Do you want to get in a room?" We were all kind of reminding each other to stay on task.

But in some companies, you're the responsible one. I learned that really quickly by working with one of my managers: if we didn't put a stake in the ground, the work simply wouldn't move forward.

### The template

After an acquisition, I had to fill out a project template. I didn't know a lot of the answers, but I had some of the materials, and I was able to fill out about 75% of it with some certainty. Maybe 10% more was a question. The rest was left blank, because it started as a blank template.

So I took my best guess on my own. Then I met with one stakeholder, and we reviewed it together and got it a little more correct. Then I took that refined template and brought it to the stakeholder group.

Because they had something to react to, they said immediately, "Oh, that's not right, can you make one adjustment?" Or, "Here's the missing value that goes there." We knocked out that task in one meeting. Total time, maybe two hours: the time I spent alone, the time with a single stakeholder, and the time with the group. Then the whole thing was done and checked off.

Another colleague of mine, a project manager, had the same exact template for a different project. She showed up to a meeting I was invited to, with a bigger stakeholder group, and the sheet was blank. That meeting went very poorly.

She was leaning on what she felt were her roles and responsibilities as a project manager, as if she didn't need to get involved with the details of the project or know what needed to happen to get it done. Her role was to move the project forward, and that's it.

I met with her later. It was unsolicited, but I told her I noticed she struggled to get that template filled out. One thing that has worked for me is to steep myself in the area a little, make my best attempt at a stake in the ground, review it with a single stakeholder, and then bring it to the group. I was able to finish my template rather quickly that way. She needed to schedule another meeting in front of a stakeholder group, which uses a lot of time from all those people.

Her point of view was that it wasn't her responsibility. I said that very well may be true, but your project template isn't filled out. It may or may not be your responsibility, but it's all of our responsibility to get the work done.

### Something should move every week

My general view on one-on-ones, especially with your manager: you have your top projects, let's say your top three to five. Week to week, something should move. You should make some progress on your top projects.

If one doesn't move for a week or two in a row, the update really needs to say why. Either it's deprioritized and why, or there's a blocker, or something else took priority. Otherwise, week over week, we should be seeing progress. This is the way you keep yourself accountable. That's what I mean when I say, don't show up empty handed.

### Doing it live is passé

In meetings, have your agenda ahead of time and post it on the invite. If you have notes for a read-ahead, send them out. It's never been easier with AI to put together some quick notes and send them to people ahead of time. Or bring a visual you prototyped to share with the group. I've done that several times.

This idea of "we're doing it live in the meeting" is becoming passé at this point. I don't think people are doing it anymore.

### Nobody asked me to build it

I've built several prototypes unprovoked. I built prototypes for [Bombas](/prototypes/bombas/) unasked for. I built prototypes for [Weight Watchers](/prototypes/ww/) unasked for. It showed that I was engaged. It showed that I was tuned in. It showed that I cared about the problems they were solving.

I think that's proof positive that with AI, with being engaged, and with caring, you can actually show up very well prepared. I'm very proud of those prototypes. They show my product mind, they show my creativity, and they show that I care about the audience I'm talking to.

### Be the change

This all goes back to engagement. When I talk to organizations about being engaged, what I mean is that we're all interested and tuned in to the work we're doing, and we're all pushing each other in the same direction so we're getting stuff done.

If we all thought about how we could be more engaged in how we interact with other teams, how we show up to meetings, how we collaborate, how we show progress, and how we prepare, it would solve a lot of problems. It would also inspire others.

I often think you have to be the change you want to see. If I want other people to prepare like I do, I should keep doing what I'm doing when I work with them, as a way to inspire them to show up the same way.

Don't show up empty handed. Put a stake in the ground.
