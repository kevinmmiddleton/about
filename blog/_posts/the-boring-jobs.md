This morning I found out my job tracker had been wrong for three weeks.

I used to have a small job that read my email every morning, found the rejections, and marked them in my tracker. Somewhere around September 9 it stopped. It ran on my laptop, and I still don't know exactly what killed it. Nothing broke loudly. The tracker simply got a little more wrong every day, and I didn't notice until my assistant mentioned a rejection and a paused role in my morning brief and I asked why they weren't in there.

So we moved the job to my Mac mini, which never sleeps, and ran a catch-up. It made more than 80 updates. Dozens of roles I thought were still open had passed on me weeks ago, a few had been paused, and a handful I'd never even logged.

That's a lot of "no" to absorb before lunch. It was also the most useful thing my assistant did all week.

### Muse found $5,350 doing the same kind of job

The same morning, Nate Jones put out [a video about Meta's Muse](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=gbbPBr3OraQ). He gave it the most boring job he had, finding the subscriptions he'd forgotten to cancel, and it found $5,350 a year. It has cancelled $1,285 of that so far. Other people in the video had it chase refunds, compare phone plans, and shop their car insurance.

His argument is that the smartest model won't automatically win. By now, most assistants are capable enough to cancel a subscription or book a table. What makes one useful is knowing your life well enough that the next ask is easier than the last one: which charge is a duplicate, which one you keep because someone in your family uses it, what you already tried.

I agree with him, and I've been living the small version of it.

### My assistant's best work this month was boring

When I [handed my blog to my assistant](/blog/we-have-muse-at-home/) last week, the fun part was the interview with Muse. The part that actually changed my month was much less exciting.

It keeps a list of every subscription I pay for, and once a week it reads my receipts and tells me what changed. It caught Apple One going up two dollars, Peacock going up almost two, and an app renewal I didn't want that was set to charge in a week. I cancelled that one with days to spare. None of that is impressive on its own. Together it's a job I was never going to do, done every week without me asking.

It is not perfect at this. On Monday it texted me about reordering trash bags, I told it I didn't need any, and on Tuesday my morning brief brought them up again. We fixed that too.

Nate cites a 2025 study in the American Economic Review that put a number on why this matters. Subscription companies make about 87% more from their existing subscribers, on average, because people don't stop to reconsider what they're paying for. A lot of businesses are counting on you never doing the boring job.

### I didn't notice when it stopped

Here's the part Nate's video doesn't get into. A boring job is only worth something if it keeps running, and when it stops, it looks exactly like a quiet week.

A job that finds nothing and a job that died both send you nothing. My rejection scanner didn't fail with an error. It just stopped showing up, and I read the silence as good news for three weeks.

So every job on my Mac mini now checks in when it runs, and if one goes quiet for longer than it should, I hear about it. If the whole Mac mini goes quiet, the database it reports to messages me directly. This is dull plumbing, and it's the only reason I trust the rest of it.

### The job that knows you holds a lot of you

To do the subscription job well, an assistant needs your receipts, your email, and some sense of who in your house uses what. Muse is honest that your conversations can be logged and reviewed by Meta, and Nate is upfront that it asks for real trust with your data.

Three weeks after Muse launched, OpenAI announced Dots at its Dev Day: always-on agents inside ChatGPT with their own computer in the cloud that learn how you work and, soon, will text you. Meta and OpenAI are now building the same assistant, which means they want the same thing from you, the context that makes it useful.

That's a fair trade for a lot of people. I made a different one. My assistant keeps its memory, its instructions, and its logs on a machine in my apartment, and I can read every file. The thinking still goes out to a model provider, but the record of my life stays home. I wrote more about why in [owning your context](/blog/owning-your-context/).

Either way, the question to ask any assistant is the same one I should have asked my rejection scanner: what did you actually do this week, and how would I know if you stopped?

What's the most boring job you'd hand off first?
